Yes. I think **this is the more interesting scientific question**.

You now have three representation states:

$$
\underbrace{I_0,S_0}_{\text{independent unimodal SSL}}
\rightarrow
\underbrace{I_{\text{seq}},S_{\text{seq}}}_{\text{SSL then cross-modal JEPA}}
$$

and separately

$$
\underbrace{I_{\text{joint}},S_{\text{joint}}}_{\text{joint cross-modal JEPA from scratch}}.
$$

Instead of asking only whether alignment gets better, ask:

> **What information is retained, discarded, transferred, or newly organized when independently learned unimodal representations are turned into cross-modal representations?**

That can absolutely become the heart of the paper.

## Think of cross-modal training as an information transformation

Very roughly, each modality begins with

$$
Z_I = [Z_{\text{shared}},Z_{\text{image-private}}]
$$

$$
Z_S = [Z_{\text{shared}},Z_{\text{spectra-private}}].
$$

Cross-modal training has an incentive to emphasize whatever is **predictable across the modalities**.

So after alignment, perhaps:

$$
Z'_I =
[\uparrow Z_{\text{shared}},
\downarrow Z_{\text{image-private}}]
$$

and similarly for spectra.

The key empirical question becomes:

> **Does cross-modal JEPA discover shared physical factors while sacrificing modality-specific information?**

And then:

> **Does sequential pretraining preserve private information better than joint-from-scratch training?**

That's a much stronger story.

---

# I would build an "information accounting" experiment

For every representation:

$$
I_0,S_0,I_{\text{seq}},S_{\text{seq}},I_{\text{joint}},S_{\text{joint}}
$$

measure four things:

1. **shared information**
2. **modality-private information**
3. **downstream physical information**
4. **representation geometry**

Then literally ask where the information goes.

---

# 1. First: build a property battery

This is probably the most important experiment.

Divide your available physical labels into three conceptual categories.

### Image-dominant information

Things that should be particularly accessible visually:

* morphology
* axis ratio / ellipticity
* concentration
* galaxy size
* Sérsic-type structural quantities
* inclination
* spiral/bar features, if available

### Spectrum-dominant information

For example:

* emission-line properties
* metallicity
* spectral age indicators
* velocity dispersion
* detailed star-formation indicators

depending on the labels you actually have.

### Shared physical information

Things both modalities plausibly carry:

* redshift
* stellar mass
* star formation rate
* broad galaxy type
* colour-related properties
* perhaps age

Then probe **all six representations** using exactly the same simple linear probe.

Your fundamental table becomes:

| Representation      | Image-private | Spectrum-private | Shared |
| ------------------- | ------------: | ---------------: | -----: |
| Image SSL           |          high |                — |   high |
| Spectrum SSL        |             — |             high |   high |
| Sequential image    |             ? |                — |      ? |
| Sequential spectrum |             — |                ? |      ? |
| Joint image         |             ? |                — |      ? |
| Joint spectrum      |             — |                ? |      ? |

But don't report only absolute scores.

Report:

$$
\Delta_{\text{align}}(y)
=
P(y\mid Z_{\text{aligned}})
-
P(y\mid Z_{\text{SSL}})
$$

for every property \(y\).

That gives you an **information gain/loss spectrum**.

Imagine the result is:

```text
                 Δ after cross-modal alignment

Redshift                  +++
Stellar mass               ++
SFR                         ++
Galaxy morphology            0
Axis ratio                  --
Fine morphology            ---
```

That's immediately interpretable:

> Cross-modal prediction enriches features supported by both modalities while suppressing features specific to one observation channel.

That would be a strong result.

---

# 2. But we can do better: explicitly separate shared and private subspaces

This is the experiment I most want you to run.

Take paired representations:

$$
Z_I \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d_I},
\qquad
Z_S \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d_S}.
$$

Run **CCA** (or regularized CCA).

CCA finds directions

$$
u_k^\top Z_I,\qquad v_k^\top Z_S
$$

that are maximally correlated across the two modalities.

You can interpret the high-correlation canonical directions as an approximation to:

$$
Z_{\text{shared}}.
$$

Everything orthogonal/not predictable becomes approximately:

$$
Z_{\text{private}}.
$$

Do this separately for:

### Before alignment

$$
(I_0,S_0)
$$

### Sequential JEPA

$$
(I_{\text{seq}},S_{\text{seq}})
$$

### Joint JEPA

$$
(I_{\text{joint}},S_{\text{joint}}).
$$

Now plot the **canonical correlation spectrum**:

$$
\rho_1,\rho_2,\ldots,\rho_d.
$$

This could be one of your main figures.

Suppose you observe:

```text
Unimodal SSL       ██████░░░░░░░░
Sequential JEPA    ██████████░░░░
Joint JEPA         █████████████░
```

Joint training has pushed far more dimensions into the shared subspace.

But that alone isn't necessarily good.

Now ask:

> **What did those additional shared dimensions replace?**

That's where the next experiment comes in.

---

# 3. Probe the shared and private components separately

This becomes extremely compelling.

After CCA, construct approximately:

$$
Z_I^{\text{shared}}
$$

and

$$
Z_I^{\text{private}}
=
Z_I-Z_I^{\text{shared}}.
$$

Same for spectra.

Then train probes.

Imagine:

| Representation component | Redshift |     Mass | Morphology | Inclination |
| ------------------------ | -------: | -------: | ---------: | ----------: |
| Image shared             | **high** | **high** |     medium |         low |
| Image private            |      low |   medium |   **high** |    **high** |

Now do this before and after multimodal training.

You could discover:

### Before alignment

$$
Z_I =
40\%\text{ shared}
+
60\%\text{ private}
$$

### Sequential

$$
Z_I =
65\%\text{ shared}
+
35\%\text{ private}
$$

### Joint

$$
Z_I =
85\%\text{ shared}
+
15\%\text{ private}.
$$

Obviously don't literally call these percentages unless you define the metric carefully, but conceptually that's what you're testing.

Then your result becomes:

> **Joint cross-modal learning creates stronger common representations, but does so by suppressing modality-specific information. Sequential alignment achieves a different compromise, increasing shared structure while preserving substantially more unimodal information.**

Now we have a paper.

---

# 4. Cross-modal predictability is another measure of "shared"

There's an even simpler version than CCA.

Train:

$$
f_{S\rightarrow I}(Z_S)\approx Z_I
$$

using ridge regression.

Then define

$$
Z_I^{\text{shared}}
=
f_{S\rightarrow I}(Z_S)
$$

and residual:

$$
Z_I^{\text{private}}
=
Z_I-\hat Z_I.
$$

Similarly:

$$
Z_S^{\text{shared}}
=f_{I\rightarrow S}(Z_I)
$$

$$
Z_S^{\text{private}}
=
Z_S-\hat Z_S.
$$

This interpretation is beautifully compatible with JEPA because it defines shared information operationally as:

> **whatever is predictable from the other modality.**

Then calculate:

$$
R^2_{S\rightarrow I}
$$

and

$$
R^2_{I\rightarrow S}.
$$

You can watch cross-predictable information grow:

$$
SSL
\rightarrow
Sequential
\rightarrow
Joint.
$$

And probe the residual.

If the residual contains morphology while predicted components contain redshift/mass, that's a gorgeous demonstration of what cross-modal JEPA is actually doing.

---

# 5. Measure forgetting directly

Now compare

$$
I_0 \rightarrow I_{\text{seq}}.
$$

There are two notions of forgetting.

### Functional forgetting

Does a piece of information become less decodable?

$$
\Delta_y=
\operatorname{Probe}(I_{\text{seq}},y)
-
\operatorname{Probe}(I_0,y).
$$

Negative means information became harder to access.

### Geometric forgetting

How much did representation geometry change?

Use:

$$
CKA(I_0,I_{\text{seq}})
$$

and

$$
CKA(S_0,S_{\text{seq}}).
$$

CKA is well suited to comparing separately trained neural representations and is widely used for this purpose. ([Proceedings of Machine Learning Research][1])

Then also calculate:

### kNN preservation

For each galaxy:

$$
N_k^{\text{before}}(x)
$$

versus

$$
N_k^{\text{after}}(x).
$$

Measure:

$$
\frac{|N_k^\text{before}(x)
\cap
N_k^\text{after}(x)|}{k}.
$$

This tells you whether the **local meaning of similarity** changes during multimodal training.

---

# 6. The really interesting question: WHICH galaxies move?

Don't just average CKA.

For each object calculate representation displacement after aligning spaces with Procrustes:

$$
d_i=
\|
Rz_i^{SSL}-z_i^{aligned}
\|.
$$

Then correlate \(d_i\) with physical characteristics.

Maybe:

* unusual spectra move more,
* peculiar morphologies move more,
* high-SFR galaxies move more,
* edge-on galaxies move more.

That tells you **where the two modalities disagree**.

This is potentially very cool scientifically.

For example:

> Galaxies whose image-derived and spectral-derived physical evidence disagree undergo the largest representation updates during cross-modal alignment.

Then cross-modal learning is doing more than compression. It's revealing **cross-modal disagreement**.

---

# 7. Cross-modal training may CREATE information accessibility

There's an important subtlety.

Cross-modal alignment cannot magically add information to the pixels that isn't there.

But it **can reorganize information that was previously entangled** so a simple downstream model can access it.

Suppose stellar mass probe:

$$
I_0: 0.61
$$

$$
I_{\text{seq}}:0.78.
$$

That doesn't necessarily mean the image suddenly contains more information about stellar mass.

It may mean the spectrum has acted as a **learning signal that linearizes that factor** in image representation space.

This gives you another distinction:

### Information content

Does the representation contain \(y\)?

versus

### Information accessibility

Can a simple decoder extract \(y\)?

You can test this with a hierarchy:

* linear probe
* 1-layer MLP
* 2-layer MLP

If cross-modal training mostly improves the linear probe while a powerful MLP was already able to extract the property from SSL features, then cross-modal learning has **reorganized** rather than truly introduced task-relevant information.

That is a really nice result.

---

# 8. This lets us ask something even cleaner about Sequential vs Joint

Now compare the two methods on a **Pareto frontier**.

x-axis:

$$
\text{cross-modal shared information}
$$

for example average canonical correlation / cross-modal \(R^2\).

y-axis:

$$
\text{retained unimodal information}.
$$

You might obtain:

```text
          ↑ retain private information

       SSL ●
           |
           |
Sequential ●──────────
                       \
                        ● Joint
                            → cross-modal shared information
```

That's potentially the **single best figure in the paper**.

The question isn't:

> Which model wins?

It's:

> **What is the trade-off between preserving modality-specific structure and acquiring modality-invariant structure?**

Perhaps:

* Joint JEPA maximizes cross-modal agreement.
* Sequential JEPA gives a better shared/private tradeoff.
* Independent SSL maximizes private structure but lacks explicit correspondence.

That's substantially more interesting.

# 9. And then CLIP fits naturally

Now CLIP becomes another point on that frontier rather than the entire paper.

You can have:

$$
\{\text{Unimodal SSL},
\text{Sequential JEPA},
\text{Joint JEPA},
\text{CLIP}\}.
$$

And characterize every model by:

### A. Pair alignment

Retrieval.

### B. Structural alignment

CKA / CCA / neighborhoods.

### C. Cross-predictability

\(I\rightarrow S\), \(S\rightarrow I\).

### D. Shared physical information

Shared-subspace probes.

### E. Private information retention

Residual probes.

Now you could get something beautiful like:

| Model           | Pair align | Shared structure | Private info |
| --------------- | ---------: | ---------------: | -----------: |
| SSL             |        low |           medium |     **high** |
| Sequential JEPA |       high |         **high** |         high |
| Joint JEPA      |   **high** |         **high** |          low |
| CLIP            |   **high** |           medium |          low |

That's a vastly more meaningful characterization than ranking them by retrieval accuracy.

# I would formulate the actual scientific question as

> ### What is the cost of multimodal agreement?

More formally:

> **When representations are optimized to encode information predictable across modalities, which properties of the original unimodal representations are preserved, emphasized, reorganized, or discarded?**

Then your second question:

> **Does learning strong unimodal representations before cross-modal alignment change this shared–private information trade-off compared with joint multimodal learning from scratch?**

And *then* JEPA vs CLIP can be question three:

> **Does predictive alignment induce a different shared–private information trade-off from contrastive alignment?**

That's a very coherent hierarchy:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Unimodal}
\xrightarrow{\text{multimodal objective}}
\text{Shared}+\text{Private}
}
$$

and you're studying how the decomposition changes as a function of:

$$
\boxed{
\text{training strategy}
\times
\text{alignment objective}.
}
$$

One thing we should be aware of: a June 2026 paper called **“When to Align, When to Predict: A Phase Diagram for Multimodal Learning”** already studies when cross-modal alignment versus prediction succeeds, including experiments on astrophysical data. ([arXiv][2]) That actually makes me **more** convinced your differentiation should be the *information transformation / shared-vs-private / sequential-vs-joint* question rather than simply “predictive JEPA versus contrastive CLIP.” Also, prior work has shown that independently trained unimodal encoders can already have surprisingly similar geometry before explicit alignment, so your **before → after** analysis has a solid conceptual precedent. ([CVF Open Access][3])

If we keep going gradually, the next thing I'd do is reduce all of this to **5–6 exact experiments/figures that you can realistically run for PriGM**, because there are now enough ideas here to accidentally turn this into a full NeurIPS paper rather than a four-page workshop submission.
