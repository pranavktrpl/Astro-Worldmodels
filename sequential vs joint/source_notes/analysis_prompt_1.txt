Yes. And I think this comparison may be **more scientifically interesting than simply “JEPA beats CLIP.”**

You effectively have two different hypotheses about *how multimodal structure should be learned*:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Unimodal learning}
\rightarrow
\text{cross-modal alignment}
}
$$

versus

$$
\boxed{
\text{representation learning + alignment jointly}
}
$$

Call them:

* **Sequential JEPA:** SSL image encoder + SSL spectrum encoder → cross-modal JEPA post-training.
* **Joint JEPA:** random initialization → image/spectrum pairs trained jointly with the cross-modal JEPA objective.

The core question becomes:

> **Should modalities first learn their own structure and then meet, or should shared structure emerge directly from paired observations?**

That is a genuinely nice representation-learning question.

## The strongest experiment: paired-data efficiency

This is the first thing I would run.

Keep your unimodal pretrained encoders fixed as the starting point for Sequential JEPA, and vary the number of **paired image–spectrum examples** used for cross-modal training:

$$
1\%,5\%,10\%,25\%,50\%,100\%.
$$

Train Joint JEPA from scratch using exactly those same pairs.

Plot:

$$
\text{cross-modal performance}
\quad\text{vs}\quad
N_{\text{paired}}.
$$

Measure retrieval, cross-modal linear predictability, physical-property probes, etc.

Suppose you get:

```text
                1%    5%    10%    25%    100%

Sequential      0.55  0.72   0.79   0.84    0.87
Joint           0.08  0.22   0.41   0.70    0.86
```

That would be a **very strong result**.

The claim isn't merely:

> Pretraining helps.

It's:

> **Learning modality-specific structure independently drastically reduces the amount of paired supervision needed to discover cross-modal structure.**

That is especially relevant because paired multimodal data is usually scarcer than unimodal data.

---

# Experiment 2: What gets destroyed during alignment?

This could be extremely interesting because you have the **before-alignment embeddings** for Sequential JEPA.

Let

$$
I_0,S_0
$$

be pretrained unimodal representations, and

$$
I_1,S_1
$$

be those same encoders after cross-modal JEPA training.

Compute:

$$
CKA(I_0,I_1)
$$

and

$$
CKA(S_0,S_1).
$$

Also do:

* kNN neighborhood preservation,
* pairwise-distance correlation,
* linear probe performance before/after,
* effective rank/eigenspectrum before/after.

You're asking:

> **How much does multimodal alignment rewrite the unimodal representation?**

You could discover something like:

$$
CKA(I_0,I_1)=0.92
$$

but

$$
CKA(S_0,S_1)=0.61.
$$

That would suggest one modality is being used as an **anchor**, while the other reorganizes itself around it.

Which leads directly to another experiment.

---

# Experiment 3: Which modality dominates the shared space?

Image ↔ spectrum is not a symmetric information relationship.

Test:

$$
I\rightarrow S
$$

versus

$$
S\rightarrow I.
$$

Fit simple held-out linear/ridge predictors:

$$
\hat S=f(I)
$$

and

$$
\hat I=g(S).
$$

Compare prediction quality for Sequential and Joint JEPA.

You may find:

$$
R^2(S\rightarrow I) \gg R^2(I\rightarrow S)
$$

or vice versa.

More importantly, compare how much **each modality changes during training**.

That can reveal:

> Cross-modal JEPA alignment is not necessarily symmetric; the more informative/easier-to-predict modality may determine the geometry of the joint representation.

That's quite interesting for multimodal representation learning generally.

---

# Experiment 4: Shared information vs modality-private information

This is probably my favourite deeper analysis.

Take image representation \(I\).

Train a simple map from spectrum:

$$
\hat I = f(S).
$$

Then define:

$$
I_{\text{shared}}=\hat I
$$

and

$$
I_{\text{private}}=I-\hat I.
$$

Very loosely, this decomposes the image representation into:

$$
I =
\underbrace{I_{\text{shared}}}_{\text{predictable from spectrum}}
+
\underbrace{I_{\text{private}}}_{\text{image-specific}}.
$$

Do the equivalent for spectra.

Then compare Sequential vs Joint JEPA.

### Hypothesis

Sequential training might produce:

$$
Z =
Z_{\text{shared}}
\oplus
Z_{\text{private}},
$$

because SSL pretraining has already learned rich modality-specific structure.

Joint JEPA may instead emphasize:

$$
Z \approx Z_{\text{shared}}
$$

because **only information useful for predicting the other modality is consistently rewarded**.

Now probe both components for properties.

For example:

| Representation   | Morphology | Redshift | Metallicity | SFR |
| ---------------- | ---------: | -------: | ----------: | --: |
| Shared component |            |          |             |     |
| Image-private    |            |          |             |     |
| Spectrum-private |            |          |             |     |

This could give you a genuinely satisfying interpretation of what the two training regimes are doing.

---

# Experiment 5: Does joint training throw away useful unimodal information?

This follows immediately.

Run identical probes on:

$$
I_{\text{SSL}},
I_{\text{Sequential}},
I_{\text{Joint}}
$$

and similarly for spectra.

Imagine:

| Model           | Cross-modal retrieval | Morphology | Redshift |
| --------------- | --------------------: | ---------: | -------: |
| SSL             |                     — |   **0.91** |     0.72 |
| Sequential JEPA |              **0.88** |       0.89 | **0.81** |
| Joint JEPA      |                  0.87 |       0.72 |     0.79 |

That is hugely informative.

Both multimodal models might align equally well, but Joint JEPA may sacrifice information that doesn't help the paired prediction objective.

You then get this principle:

> **Cross-modal alignment quality is not equivalent to representation quality.**

Very PriGM.

---

# Experiment 6: freeze vs fine-tune

You should almost certainly add this control.

Have:

$$
\boxed{\text{SSL → Frozen → Alignment head}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{SSL → Full fine-tuning}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Joint from scratch}}
$$

This separates two explanations.

### Frozen does well

Then the two SSL spaces were **already structurally compatible**, and you merely needed to discover the mapping between them.

That's fascinating.

### Frozen performs badly but fine-tuning works

Then cross-modal learning must actually **reorganize the representations**.

This gives you a remarkably clean experiment:

> How much representational plasticity is actually necessary for multimodal alignment?

You can even gradually unfreeze:

* projector only,
* last layer,
* last 25%,
* last 50%,
* all layers.

Then plot alignment performance against trainable parameters / CKA change.

---

# Experiment 7: noisy pairing

This is a really good inductive-bias test.

Artificially corrupt:

$$
0,5,10,20,40\%
$$

of image-spectrum pairs.

Compare Sequential and Joint.

My hypothesis would be that pretrained representations act as a prior:

$$
\text{strong unimodal structure}
+
\text{noisy alignment signal}
$$

might be more robust than learning everything from the noisy pair relation.

If Sequential JEPA degrades much more slowly, you can argue:

> **Unimodal pretraining regularizes multimodal alignment against imperfect correspondence.**

Very generalizable result.

---

# Experiment 8: alignment dynamics

Save checkpoints during cross-modal training and make curves for:

$$
\text{cross-modal retrieval}
$$

$$
\text{cross-modal CKA}
$$

$$
\text{unimodal CKA to initialization}
$$

$$
\text{linear probe performance}
$$

against training steps.

You might see Sequential JEPA do:

```text
good unimodal geometry
        ↓
rapid cross-modal alignment
        ↓
small representation movement
```

whereas Joint JEPA does:

```text
random
  ↓
learn modality structure
  ↓
discover shared factors
  ↓
align
```

This would make a beautiful figure.

---

# There's one critical fairness issue

You **cannot** simply report:

> Sequential JEPA = 90%
> Joint JEPA = 80%
> therefore sequential training is better.

Because Sequential JEPA has already consumed additional:

* data,
* optimization steps,
* compute.

You need to disentangle **initialization/pretraining** from **total compute**.

So ideally have at least:

| Model             | Unimodal SSL | Paired training |
| ----------------- | ------------ | --------------- |
| Joint             | ❌            | ✓               |
| Sequential frozen | ✓            | head only       |
| Sequential FT     | ✓            | ✓               |

And when possible report:

* paired samples consumed,
* total samples consumed,
* paired training compute,
* total compute.

Your strongest conclusion will likely be about **paired-data efficiency**, rather than absolute computational efficiency.

# If I were choosing only four experiments

Given the PriGM paper constraint, I wouldn't run everything.

I'd build the paper around:

### **1. Pair-efficiency curves**

The main result.

$$
\boxed{\text{How many paired samples are required?}}
$$

### **2. Representation preservation**

CKA + kNN before/after alignment.

$$
\boxed{\text{How much does alignment rewrite each modality?}}
$$

### **3. Shared/private information**

Cross-modal linear prediction residual + probes.

$$
\boxed{\text{What information survives joint learning?}}
$$

### **4. Frozen vs fine-tuned alignment**

Tests whether existing unimodal spaces are already compatible.

$$
\boxed{\text{Does multimodal learning require representation rewriting?}}
$$

Those four together tell one coherent story.

# And I think this changes the paper framing

Instead of merely:

> **CLIP vs JEPA for astronomical multimodal learning**

you could frame the JEPA side around:

# **When Should Modalities Meet? Sequential vs Joint Predictive Multimodal Representation Learning**

The conceptual setup is:

$$
\textbf{Sequential:}\qquad
X\rightarrow Z_X,
\quad
Y\rightarrow Z_Y
\qquad\text{then}\qquad
Z_X\leftrightarrow Z_Y
$$

versus

$$
\textbf{Joint:}\qquad
(X,Y)
\rightarrow
(Z_X,Z_Y)
$$

simultaneously.

And the underlying question becomes:

> **Does effective multimodal representation learning require jointly discovering the modality representations, or can independently learned representations be efficiently composed through predictive alignment?**

This also slots beautifully into the earlier CLIP-vs-JEPA idea. You effectively get a **2×2 conceptual grid**:

|                 | **Joint learning** | **Pretrain → align**            |
| --------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| **Contrastive** | CLIP-style         | pretrained CLIP-style alignment |
| **Predictive**  | Joint X-JEPA       | Sequential X-JEPA               |

You don't necessarily have to train all four. But that grid gives the paper a much broader conceptual framework.

And if your existing result is that **SSL-pretrained → JEPA alignment works much better with few pairs than joint-from-scratch JEPA**, I would seriously consider making *that*, rather than CLIP-vs-JEPA, the main paper story. It asks a cleaner principles question: **what should be learned before modalities are forced to communicate?**
